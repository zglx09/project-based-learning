Minnie Vautrin

The Goddess at the Rape of Nanking

The life of Minnie Vautrin

  • Born into a blacksmith's family as the second of three children in the tiny village of Secor, Clayton County, Illinois on September 27, 1886.
  • Despite her family's financially disadvantaged background, Ms. Vautrin worked hard to pave her own way, juggling between her household chores, part-time jobs, and excelling in her studies.
  • Ms. Vautrin enrolled, and later graduated top of her class from Illinois State Normal University with a Degree in Education in 1907, before continuing her studies again at the University of Illinois.
  • In 1912, Ms. Vautrin joined the Foreign Christian Missionary Society and was sent to China to promote education for women by developing a girl's school.
  • Ms. Vautrin went on to further her studies, attaining a Master's degree in Education before returning to China to become the acting president at Ginling College in 1919, establishing free clinics and elementary schools for the poor.
  • In July of 1937, the Japanese army invaded Nanking, and while many attempted to leave the country, Ms. Vautrin insisted on remaining in Nanking to help the civilians.
  • She converted Ginling College into a shelter for women and children, tirelessly caring and protecting them from the rampaging Japanese soldiers despite facing threats herself.
  • Unfortunately, the physical and mental strain caused her health to deteriorate and she had to return to America in May 1940 for medical treatment.
  • Sadly, only a year later, in May 1941, Ms. Vautrin ended her own life while alone in a friend's apartment, leaving a note saying that her life was a "failure".

“To us, she was not just a missionary. She was a guardian angel, a mother to thousands, shielding us from the horrors outside the walls of Ginling College.”

— From a Nanjing Massacre survivor
